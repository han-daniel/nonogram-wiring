Introduction

Constructing Chain of Reasoning

Cells are named (row, column), counted from the top left: (2,3) is row 2, column 3.

1Each wire is a line-based strategy

A strategy reads one row or column. From the clue and the cells already known on that line, it forces one more cell, and each known cell it used becomes a wire into that cell. Every example below is row 1.

2Boards are solved in levels

Level 0 is what the clues force on their own. Level 1 is what level 0 then forces, and so on until the board is full. The graph gives each level a column, as the chart does. Click a cell to see what it wires to, or a level to light up its cells.

3Wiring can come from a later level

A cell’s level is the earliest it can be forced, but a student can wire it from other cells already known, including cells from later levels. Click a cell to step through its wirings, or a level to light up its cells.

4Some order may not matter

This is the wiring behind (5,4) on nonogram 1. A cell has to come after the cells wired into it. Nothing else about the order is fixed, and every order that respects the wires builds this same wiring. Click the cells in the order you would decide them.

5The chart opens on the wiring built from the most recent mark

A mark can often be wired more than one way, and the record does not show which one the student used. The chart opens on the wiring that uses the most recent mark, since the last thing written is the likeliest prompt for the next. Wirings that rest only on unwritten ×s come after those. The rest stay one click away.

On 4×4 b from Demo, a student may write (2,1), (3,1), (4,2), then (3,2). (3,2) has six wirings. Both (3,1) and (4,2) force it, and (4,2) is the more recent, so its wiring opens first. The other four rest only on unwritten ×s, and the first of them uses ×(1,2).
